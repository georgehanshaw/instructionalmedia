Same diagnosis, different people

Explore how neurodevelopmental conditions vary from person to person, then practice fact-checking what you see online.

One diagnosis, three different people

These three fictional people each have ADHD. Look at what shows up for each, then choose Add a good-fit support to see how the right match changes daily life. They are illustrative composites, not real people or data.

Marcus, age 8

Second grade

What you might see:

  • Leaves his seat during seatwork and blurts out answers.
  • Has trouble waiting his turn at recess.
  • Struggles at home and at school, not in one place only.
Daily frictionHigh
Predictable routines, movement breaks, and parent training in behavior strategies give Marcus structure that fits how he works.

What a stereotype misses: This is more than “a busy boy.” A diagnosis looks at whether symptoms last, show up in more than one setting, and interfere with learning and friendships.

Priya, age 15

Tenth grade

What you might see:

  • Seems to daydream in class and rarely disrupts anyone.
  • Loses homework she actually finished.
  • Works much harder than peers to look “fine.”
Daily frictionHigh
An evaluation that looks for the inattentive presentation, plus planning tools and extended time, helps Priya show what she knows.

What a stereotype misses: No hyperactivity does not mean no ADHD. The inattentive presentation is often noticed later, especially in girls.

Elena, age 34

Project coordinator

What you might see:

  • Restlessness is mostly inner, not visible.
  • Misses deadlines despite real effort.
  • Thrives in a crisis and with new ideas.
Daily frictionHigh
Workplace accommodations, coaching, and a conversation with a clinician about treatment options help Elena use her strengths.

What a stereotype misses: Adults can have ADHD that was never recognized in childhood. Real strengths and real impairments can exist together.

Takeaway: Two people with the same diagnosis can look very different by age, strengths, environment, and support. That is why diagnosis rests on careful evaluation of the whole person, not on a familiar stereotype.

Three habits to carry into Discussion 2

  1. Check the source. Who is speaking, what are their credentials, and do they point to evidence or an evaluation?
  2. Separate symptom from pattern. Relating to a symptom is not meeting criteria. Ask how long, how often, and how much it interferes with life.
  3. Respond with humility. People’s experiences vary. Absolute claims and “diagnose yourself” quizzes are warning signs.

Reflect: Think of one post or video you saw this week. Which of the three habits would change how you read it?
