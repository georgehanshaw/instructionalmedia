Lesson outline

Stages of Faith: overview

In this module we will explore James W. Fowler's Stages of Faith.

His theory posits that people experience six different stages as their faith matures.

This theory can be applied to traditional faiths such as Judaism, Islam, and Christianity, as well as alternative worldviews and spiritualities.

Think of the stages as a climb up a mountain. Before Stage 1, Fowler describes a pre-stage called primal faith in infancy, shown as point 0 at the base.

How to read the stages

The stages describe how a person arrives at and applies beliefs, not what they believe. They are not a scale for measuring a person's worth, and people can find wholeness and integrity at any stage.

Choose where to begin

Move through the stages in order with the Next button, or jump to one:

Primal Faith

Pre-stage (infancy)

Faith begins in infancy, before words, as a feeling rather than an idea.

Key ideas

  • In this pre-stage, the seeds of trust, courage, hope, and love are planted.
  • Trust grows when caregivers respond reliably to a child's needs.
  • There are no beliefs to explain yet. The child simply experiences whether the world feels safe.

A family in a cave home

  1. A young child in a cave home babbles happily: “goo goo ga ga.”
  2. The child cries out.
  3. A caregiver soothes the child: “It's okay.”
  4. A caregiver says, “He's back,” as another family member returns, and the child is safe and cared for again.

When comfort arrives reliably, a child learns that the world can be trusted. That early trust becomes the foundation for later faith.

Stage 1: Intuitive-Projective

Stage 1 of 6

As thought and language emerge, imagination and feeling shape a child's first ideas about faith.

Key ideas

  • Faith is shaped by feelings and perception: what a child sees, hears, and senses.
  • Children begin to develop moral emotions and standards.
  • Children take in the stories, symbols, and practices of the adults and families around them.

Story time

  1. A group of children sit together, listening to a story. Their imaginations carry them into it.
  2. Feelings + perception: what the children feel and notice shapes what they come to believe.
  3. Moral emotions + standards: the children begin to feel what is fair, kind, or wrong.
  4. They also watch families and adults pray, read, and worship in many different traditions, and they absorb what they see.

Stage 2: Mythic-Literal

Stage 2 of 6

Children adopt the beliefs, moral rules, and attitudes of their community and understand them literally.

Key ideas

  • Stories and myths are used to explain experience.
  • The world is seen as based on fairness.
  • God may be pictured as having personal, physical, bodily characteristics.
  • This stage typically appears in the elementary-school years, although some adults remain at this stage.

A day at school

  1. A child with a backpack arrives in a classroom.
  2. A picture appears of a unicorn riding a bicycle.
  3. A teacher shows another picture: a creature that is half cat and half fish.
  4. At this stage, children tend to take stories like these literally, and they use stories and myths to make sense of the world.

Picture a creature that is half cat and half fish. How might a child at the Mythic-Literal stage understand a story about it?

Likely answer: literally, as a real creature exactly as described. Children at this stage take stories, symbols, and rules at face value.

Stage 3: Synthetic-Conventional

Stage 3 of 6

Most people move to this stage at adolescence. Faith becomes a shared, unexamined package that holds a community together.

Key ideas

  • Authority is outside the self: custom and tradition, friends and family, and holders of official leadership roles.
  • Faith is felt deeply and defended, but it is not analyzed. It is “just there, like water for fish.”
  • Faith is seen as a total package that everyone shares.
  • Symbols and rituals are themselves sacred.
  • It is a conformist, “don't-rock-the-boat” stage, and many adults remain in it.

Finding a place to belong

  1. An adolescent begins thinking about how they experience life, and how the pieces of their world fit together.
  2. Their faith comes from family and community. Authority sits outside themselves, in custom, tradition, and trusted leaders.
  3. Faith feels like one complete package that everyone shares. It is not taken apart or questioned.
  4. The symbols of the tradition, such as a cross, candles, or a sacred scroll, are felt to be sacred in themselves.

Stage 4: Individuative-Reflective

Stage 4 of 6

A person begins to examine the beliefs they inherited and to take responsibility for their own.

Key ideas

  • Often triggered by leaving home (emotionally or physically), reaching midlife, or a big change in a relationship or job.
  • Authority moves inside the self. The person no longer lets other people define their identity.
  • Meanings and values are examined with critical thinking.
  • The meaning of a symbol is separated from the symbol itself.
  • The change can feel frightening, disorienting, and painful, and it can last several years.

Questions arise

  1. A young adult realizes she has questions: “Why do I believe what I believe?”
  2. She works through the maze of beliefs herself, no longer relying only on outside authorities.
  3. She examines ideas, documents, and practices one at a time, using critical thinking.
  4. Friends and family who have not felt any need to change may call this “backsliding,” even though it can be part of growth.

Stage 5: Conjunctive

Stage 5 of 6

A person puts faith back together with a wider view, comfortable with complexity and open to other traditions.

Key ideas

  • This move rarely happens before midlife.
  • The person sees how things are interrelated and can hold both sides, or many sides, of an issue at once.
  • Past experiences are reclaimed and reworked.
  • Symbols, myths, and rituals are appreciated again, with deeper meaning.
  • The person is open to truth in other traditions while staying confident in their own.

Putting it back together

  1. A person looks at the bigger picture and sees how many parts of life are connected.
  2. She revisits her past and sees both sides, or many sides, of important questions.
  3. She appreciates the symbols, stories, and rituals of many traditions, including her own.
  4. She stays confident in her own tradition while expecting that truth may also be found in others.

Stage 6: Universalizing

Stage 6 of 6

Very few people reach this stage. Their lives are marked by devotion to a love and justice that reach beyond any one group.

Key ideas

  • Faith is lived out as devotion to others rather than self-fulfillment.
  • Fowler describes these people as “spending and being spent” to make real the vision they hold.
  • They are so rare that finding one in a congregation is very unlikely.

A fork in the road

  1. A signpost offers two directions: “Self-fulfillment” and “Devoted.”
  2. A person at this stage chooses devotion, giving herself to the needs of others.
  3. Along the “Devoted” road are people in need of care: an older couple, a person in a wheelchair, and others.
  4. Few people reach this stage, but it shows where the climb can lead.
